## What the Store Page Actually Advertises

The official Steam listing is the authority here, and it is fairly specific about four things:

- **A fallen kingdom premise.** The Mad King used forbidden magic to try to resuscitate the queen, lost his sanity, and the kingdom fell. This is the framing for the campaign, not a mechanical promise.
- **Horde combat in brawler style.** You slay large groups of enemies using a side-scrolling beat'em'up control scheme.
- **Deep roguelite elements.** Runs are structured with roguelite progression rather than a purely linear stage-by-stage arcade ladder.
- **Hero upgrades and multiple skills.** You upgrade heroes and unlock skills. The page describes this as a system, not a fixed list.

The store also frames the whole thing as a beat'em'up that combines old-school controls and a retro feel with 3D mechanics. That combination is the core identity claim, and it is the most useful thing to hold onto when you evaluate the game.

What the page does **not** publish: a complete hero roster, a skill tree, a boss list, damage values, unlock levels, or drop rates. If a guide presents any of those as developer-confirmed data, treat that claim with suspicion. The in-game codex is the authority for anything numeric, and the Steam page is the authority for what the game claims to be.

## Reading "Redemption" as a Design Idea, Not Just a Title

The word redemption in the title does a lot of work, and it is easy to over-read. The store description gives you the Mad King's motive — reviving the queen through forbidden magic — and the consequence — madness and a fallen kingdom. It does not describe a redemption mechanic, a morality system, a forgiveness meter, or a branching ending where the king is saved.

So how should you reason about it? Treat redemption as the thematic frame that justifies the roguelite loop. You are pushing back into a kingdom that a grieving king broke, and the structure of the game — repeated runs, accumulating upgrades, unlocking skills — mirrors an attempt to undo something that was done. That is interpretation, not documentation, and it is worth being clear about the difference.

If a redemption mechanic does exist in a fuller form, the way to verify it is inside the game itself: check the codex, check whether any end-of-run screen references the king's fate, and watch the official page for updates. In Early Access, systems can change, and the developer has not promised a specific narrative payoff in the store text.

## How to Approach Runs Without Invented Numbers

Because no official damage values, drop rates, or unlock thresholds are published, any "optimal" path you read is either community-derived or fabricated. What you can do instead is reason from the advertised structure.

A beat'em'up with roguelite elements typically asks you to balance immediate survivability against long-term scaling. If a run is going badly, banking progress toward a hero upgrade may matter more than chasing a risky encounter. If a run is going well, pushing deeper before cashing out is usually the higher-value play. Those are general principles, not Mad King Redemption specifics, and you should test them against what the game actually rewards.

Watch the retro-feel 3D mechanics claim closely, too. A brawler that mixes old-school controls with 3D spatial mechanics often has hitbox and depth behavior that differs from pure 2D arcade brawlers. Learning how the game handles vertical positioning and attack reach is likely more valuable early than any upgrade choice. That is a direct consequence of the advertised design, not a claim about specific frame data.
