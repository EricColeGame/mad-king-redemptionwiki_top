## What the Store Page Actually Advertises

The Steam listing describes a kingdom fallen to the Mad King, forbidden magic used to resuscitate the queen, and the king losing his sanity. From there, the advertised feature set is short and specific:

- Brawler-style combat, slaying hordes of enemies
- Deep roguelite elements
- Upgrading heroes and unlocking multiple skills
- A beat'em'up that combines old-school controls and a retro feel with 3D mechanics

That is the verified scope. Notably absent: a complete hero roster, a skill tree, a boss list, damage values, unlock levels, or a stated number of runs, stages, or endings. If a search result promises you "all 12 heroes" or a full skill tree breakdown, treat it as unverified. The store page does not publish those details, and the game is in Early Access, where systems can still change.

## Roguelite Structure Meets Fixed Beat'em'up Combat

The interesting tension in Mad King Redemption is the pairing itself. Classic beat'em'ups are linear and readable: you learn an enemy wave, you learn your combo, you finish the stage. Roguelite structure interrupts that by asking you to build power across attempts rather than inside a single one.

The store description supports both halves without explaining how they're stitched together. It says hordes, it says roguelite elements, it says hero upgrades and skill unlocks. What it doesn't say is whether upgrades persist fully, partially, or only unlock options that then appear as in-run choices. That distinction changes how you should play.

Practically, the way to find out is not a guide — it's your first few hours. Finish a run, then check whether the hero you used is measurably stronger, or whether you merely have new options available at the start. That answer determines whether you should be pushing deep on one hero or spreading attempts across the roster. If you want the authoritative answer rather than an inference, the in-game codex is the place to look, since the store page doesn't document it.

Two facts should shape that decision, and neither is a score.

Second, the advertised foundation is concrete enough to evaluate.
